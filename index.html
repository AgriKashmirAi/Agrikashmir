01 • ROOTSTOCKS

Understanding Apple Rootstocks

Rootstocks influence tree size, vigour, anchorage, early bearing and orchard management.

M9 — Dwarfing Rootstock

Characteristics

  • Strongly dwarfing rootstock.
  • Widely used in intensive apple orchards.
  • Encourages early bearing under suitable management.
  • Usually needs permanent support due to limited anchorage.
  • Requires careful water and nutrient management.

Management

Use a suitable trellis, maintain soil moisture and monitor tree growth regularly.
M26 — Semi-Dwarfing Rootstock
  • Produces larger trees than M9.
  • Can offer a balance between vigour and productivity.
  • Support needs depend on orchard conditions.
  • Requires attention to soil drainage and collar rot risk.
MM106 — Semi-Vigorous Rootstock
  • Generally produces larger trees than dwarfing rootstocks.
  • Needs spacing appropriate to its vigour.
  • Soil drainage is an important consideration.
  • Can be vulnerable to collar rot in poorly drained soils.
Rootstock Comparison
RootstockVigourKey consideration

Rootstock suitability depends on soil, elevation, drainage, irrigation, variety and local recommendations. Always verify planting material before purchase.

03 • PLANTATION

Plantation & Orchard Spacing

Good planning begins before the first tree is planted.

Land Selection and Preparation
  • Evaluate soil depth, texture and fertility.
  • Check drainage and water availability.
  • Assess slope, sunlight and frost exposure.
  • Conduct soil testing before nutrient application.
  • Plan access paths and irrigation before planting.
Understanding Planting Density

Planting density is the number of trees established per unit area.

Trees per hectare = 10,000 ÷ (row spacing × tree spacing)

Example: 3 m row spacing × 1 m tree spacing = approximately 3,333 trees per hectare.

This is only a mathematical example, not a recommendation for every orchard.

Planting Procedure
  • Purchase healthy, certified nursery plants.
  • Confirm variety and rootstock identity.
  • Follow the planned row layout.
  • Keep the graft union above the soil level.
  • Avoid root damage and water appropriately.
  • Establish tree support where required.
Spacing depends on rootstock, variety, soil fertility, elevation, training system and local technical recommendations.

Why is a trellis important?
  • Supports slender trees and central leaders.
  • Helps manage crop load.
  • Maintains the planned tree row.
  • Supports systematic pruning and training.
  • Helps manage canopy structure.
Pole dimensions, wire specifications and anchoring must be designed for soil conditions, wind exposure, row length and expected crop load.
05 • ORCHARD MANAGEMENT

Training, Pruning & Canopy Management

Balanced tree architecture helps improve light distribution and orchard management.

Central Leader System

A central leader system maintains one dominant vertical trunk with well-distributed lateral branches.

  • Maintain a strong central leader.
  • Develop suitably positioned lateral branches.
  • Manage competing upright shoots.
  • Maintain light penetration into the canopy.
  • Use suitable ties and supports for young trees.
Winter Pruning
  • Remove dead, damaged and diseased branches.
  • Manage excessive and competing growth.
  • Maintain the planned canopy structure.
  • Use clean, sharp pruning tools.
  • Avoid unnecessary removal of productive wood.
Summer Pruning

Summer pruning may be used to manage vigorous shoots and improve light distribution. Its timing and intensity should match the cultivar, tree age and orchard condition.

06 • WATER & NUTRITION

Irrigation & Fertigation

Healthy roots need balanced water and nutrients.

Drip Irrigation
  • Delivers water close to the root zone.
  • Can improve water-use efficiency.
  • Requires regular inspection of emitters and filters.
  • Monitor soil moisture rather than relying only on a fixed schedule.
  • Adjust irrigation according to weather and tree growth.
Fertigation

Fertigation is the application of suitable soluble nutrients through an irrigation system.

  • Base nutrient decisions on soil and leaf analysis.
  • Use compatible, water-soluble fertilizers.
  • Follow recommended concentrations and application timings.
  • Monitor pH, water quality and emitter performance.
  • Avoid over-fertilisation.
Important Nutrients
  • Nitrogen: Supports vegetative growth.
  • Phosphorus: Involved in energy transfer and root functions.
  • Potassium: Important for fruit development and plant regulation.
  • Calcium: Important for fruit firmness and tissue integrity.
  • Boron and Zinc: Essential micronutrients involved in plant growth and reproductive processes.
Fertilizer quantities must be based on soil tests, leaf analysis, tree age, crop load and local horticulture recommendations.
07 • PLANT HEALTH

Apple Diseases & Pest Management

Early identification and integrated management protect orchard health.

🍂

Apple Scab

Fungal disease that can cause dark, scabby lesions on leaves and fruit.

🌫️

Powdery Mildew

Can produce white powdery growth on leaves and young shoots.

🌳

Canker

Can cause damaged bark, sunken lesions and branch dieback.

🐛

Codling Moth

Larvae damage apples by tunnelling into fruit.

🐜

Aphids

Sap-feeding insects that can distort young leaves and shoots.

🔬

Mites

Small pests that can damage leaves and affect tree health.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM)
  • Regularly inspect leaves, shoots, branches and fruit.
  • Maintain orchard sanitation.
  • Remove and manage infected plant material appropriately.
  • Monitor pest populations and disease conditions.
  • Encourage beneficial organisms where appropriate.
  • Use only locally approved plant-protection products according to label directions and expert advice.

09 • POST-HARVEST

Harvesting, Grading & Storage

Good post-harvest practices help preserve fruit quality.

Harvesting
  • Determine maturity using appropriate maturity indicators.
  • Harvest carefully to avoid bruising.
  • Handle fruit gently and use clean picking containers.
  • Avoid harvesting solely by fruit colour.
Grading and Storage
  • Sort fruit by size, colour and quality.
  • Separate damaged and diseased fruit.
  • Use suitable packaging.
  • Maintain recommended storage temperature and humidity.
  • Monitor storage conditions regularly.

November – December
